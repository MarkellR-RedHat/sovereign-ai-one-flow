Governance: MaaS Subscriptions as the Entry Point

First principle: no AI consumption without governance.

In our stack, that starts with Model-as-a-Service subscriptions. You want access to Claude or Llama or whatever? You don't just spin up a pod and start hitting endpoints. You subscribe to a tier — Free, Premium, Enterprise — and you get an API key tied to a quota.

What is MaaS? Model-as-a-Service is exactly what it sounds like — models exposed as managed services with subscriptions, quotas, and SLAs. Think "you get 100K tokens/month on the Free tier, 10M on Premium, unlimited on Enterprise." It's how you productionize model access without everyone deploying their own inference stack.

This is self-service, but it's governed self-service. Developers get their API keys instantly. They don't need to file a ticket or wait for procurement. But platform teams define the tiers, set the quotas, and enforce the limits. Finance knows exactly what consumption is allowed before it happens.

Governance isn't a gate you walk through once. It's the foundation everything else builds on.

Isolation: Multi-Tenant Boundaries That Actually Hold

Once you have governance, you need isolation. Because if three teams are sharing one cluster, you can't have Team A's model inference spilling into Team B's GPU pool, or Team C's logs exposing Team A's prompts.

We're doing Kubernetes-native multi-tenancy the right way. Hard network policy boundaries between tenants. GPU isolation so your accelerators are dedicated to the workload that requested them. Namespace-level RBAC so nobody sees what they shouldn't.

This isn't soft isolation where you trust everyone to stay in their lane. This is enforced at the infrastructure level. If you're in Tenant A, you literally cannot reach Tenant B's services. The API server won't let you. The network won't route you. The scheduler won't give you their GPUs.

Multi-tenancy in Kubernetes: True isolation means separate namespaces, network policies that block cross-tenant traffic, resource quotas that prevent noisy neighbors, and RBAC that enforces who can do what. OpenShift adds another layer with project-level isolation and SCC (Security Context Constraints) that lockdown what containers can actually do at runtime.

Why does this matter? Because without isolation, you can't safely run production AI workloads on shared infrastructure. You end up over-provisioning — one cluster per team, one GPU pool per project — and your utilization tanks. With real isolation, you can pack workloads tight and still sleep at night.

Transparency: Show Me The Money

Alright, you've got governance and isolation. Now you need visibility.

How many tokens did Team A use last month? What did that cost? Which models are they hitting? What use cases are driving the spend? If you can't answer those questions in real-time, you don't have a production AI platform. You have a cost center waiting to explode.

Our observability layer gives you per-tenant token usage, broken down by model, by use case, by time window. You want to know what Claude 3.5 Sonnet cost you in June? Done. You want to see which team is burning through Premium quota fastest? Two clicks.

And here's the kicker: it's all tied back to the MaaS subscriptions from step one. The same tenant boundaries that enforce isolation also define the observability scope. The same quotas that govern access also drive the cost attribution. It's one coherent system.

Finance gets CSV exports for chargeback. Engineering gets Grafana dashboards for capacity planning. Leadership gets the "Show Me The Money" view that answers the one question that actually matters: what is AI costing us, and what are we getting for it?

Safety: Guardrails at the Gateway

Transparency without safety is just watching the fire burn.

You can see all the tokens flowing through your system. You can track every API call, every model invocation, every dollar spent. But if you're not testing for vulnerabilities and blocking bad requests at runtime, you're one prompt injection away from a very bad day.

That's where Garak and NeMo Guardrails come in.

What is Garak? Garak is an LLM vulnerability scanner. It throws hundreds of adversarial prompts at your models — jailbreaks, prompt injections, data leakage attempts — and reports what breaks. Think of it as a fuzzer for language models. You run it in staging before you ship. You run it continuously in production to catch drift.

In our flow, Garak runs against staging endpoints before anything goes live. You're deploying a new model? Fine. But it doesn't hit production until Garak gives it a clean bill of health. Found a vulnerability? Fix it, re-test, then ship.

And in production, NeMo Guardrails sits at the gateway. It's not just logging requests — it's actively filtering them. Block PII. Enforce topic boundaries. Prevent jailbreaks in real-time. Your governance policies aren't just documentation. They're enforced in the request path.

Security becomes a loop, not a checkbox. Test in staging. Monitor in production. Feed findings back into policy. Rinse and repeat.

The Full Flow: Governance → Isolation → Transparency → Safety → Sovereign AI

Let's bring it all together. Here's what the full flow looks like:

A developer on Team A wants to build a RAG application. They subscribe to the Premium MaaS tier, get an API key, and start coding. The subscription enforces governance — they have 10M tokens/month, and when they hit that limit, the API starts returning 429s. No surprises.

Their workload runs in Tenant A's namespace. It can't see Team B's data. It can't consume Team C's GPUs. Isolation is automatic and enforced.

As they develop, platform engineers watch token usage climb on the observability dashboard. They can see it's Claude 3.5 Sonnet, hitting the embeddings endpoint, processing internal docs. Finance sees the same data in their monthly chargeback report. Transparency is real-time and multi-stakeholder.

Before the app goes to production, Garak runs a security sweep. Finds one prompt injection vector. Team A patches it. Garak re-tests. Clean. The app ships.

In production, NeMo Guardrails watches every request. If someone tries to trick the model into leaking PII, the request gets blocked. If usage spikes unexpectedly, alerts fire. Safety is continuous.

That's sovereign AI in one flow. Governance, isolation, transparency, and safety — all through one control plane, using one set of primitives, managed by one platform team.
